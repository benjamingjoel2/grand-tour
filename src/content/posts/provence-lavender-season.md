It starts before you see it. Somewhere on the long, straight road across the plateau, with the windows down because the air conditioning has given up arguing with the heat, a smell drifts in: warm, resinous, faintly medicinal, sweeter than you expect. Then the fields open up on both sides, row after combed row of violet running towards a line of blue hills, each stripe separated by a ribbon of pale, stony earth. Bees drone in the flowers loudly enough to hear over the engine. A lone stone farmhouse sits in the middle of it all, exactly as it does on the postcards, and for once the postcard turns out to have been understating things.

Lavender season is Provence at its most theatrical, and also at its busiest and hottest. Getting it right is mostly about timing, a little about manners, and a lot about where you choose to sleep.

## When the lavender actually flowers

There is no fixed date, and anyone who promises one is guessing. The flowering moves with the weather each year, and it moves with altitude, which is the key to planning a trip around it.

- **The Valensole plateau** flowers first. The local tourism board gives a window of roughly [mid-June to mid-July, depending on the weather](https://en.tourisme-alpes-haute-provence.com/grands-espaces-naturels/4860347_curiosite-naturelle-valensole-lavender-and-lavandin/), and harvesting can begin before the end of June, so the plateau's peak is short.
- **Sault and its plateau**, higher up and further west, come later. The regional tourism board says the lavender around Sault [starts flowering in July and lasts into August](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/en/provence/things-to-do/outdoor-activities/5-spots-to-admire-lavender-in-bloom/), and the same page notes that across the region, timing shifts each year with rainfall and temperature.
- **Around Avignon and Aix-en-Provence**, the same source suggests early July as the best moment.

Our advice is simple: if you can only come once, aim for the first half of July and base yourself somewhere that lets you reach both plateaux. If Valensole has been cut, Sault will usually still be in flower; if Sault is not yet out, Valensole will be. Check the tourist office pages a week or two before you travel rather than trusting last year's photographs.

## The Valensole plateau

Valensole is the image most people have in mind: wide, open, almost prairie-like farmland in the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence, with lavender and wheat alternating across a landscape the regional tourism board puts at [more than 800 km²](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/en/provence/things-to-do/outdoor-activities/5-spots-to-admire-lavender-in-bloom/). In early summer the wheat turns gold while the lavender turns violet, and the contrast is the whole point.

Much of what you see here is actually lavandin, a hybrid of true lavender and spike lavender. The Valensole tourism page explains that [lavandin yields up to ten times more essential oil than traditional lavender](https://en.tourisme-alpes-haute-provence.com/grands-espaces-naturels/4860347_curiosite-naturelle-valensole-lavender-and-lavandin/), which is why it dominates the big commercial fields; you'll recognise it by its long, compact flower spikes. True lavender, finer and more fragrant, prefers higher ground.

The plateau rewards slowness. Rather than driving the same main road as every coach, take the smaller lanes that peel off between the fields. Go early in the morning or in the last two hours before sunset: the light is softer, the colours deeper, and the heat bearable. The middle of the day is flat, glaring and crowded.

## Sault and the Ventoux side

Sault feels completely different. The village sits on [a rocky promontory and looks down over a wide cultivated plain of lavender, lavandin and einkorn wheat](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/que-faire/culture-et-patrimoine/lieux/le-village-de-sault-sault-fr-2880569/), a patchwork that shifts colour through the summer. The bulk of Mont Ventoux rises to the north-west, and the air up here is noticeably fresher than down in the valleys.

If you want to walk among the fields rather than look at them from a lay-by, the [Chemin des Lavandes at Sault is a signposted 4 km loop](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/en/provence/things-to-do/outdoor-activities/5-spots-to-admire-lavender-in-bloom/) with information panels on lavender growing and distillation. Take water and a hat; there is little shade.

Sault also hosts the region's best-known lavender festival, the [Fête de la Lavande, held every year on 15 August](https://www.fetedelalavande.fr/), with a scythe-cutting contest and a producers' market. It is a cheerful, genuinely local day out, but expect crowds and arrive early, and check the festival's own site for the current programme, parking and shuttle details.

## Sénanque abbey

The view down onto Notre-Dame de Sénanque, a pale stone abbey in a narrow valley below Gordes with lavender rows lined up in front of its church, is one of the most photographed in France. It is worth remembering that it is a working monastery, not a backdrop.

The abbey's own visitor page is clear on the rules:

- [Access to the lavender fields is forbidden](https://www.senanque.fr/visite-de-labbaye/), to protect the community's harvest.
- Visitors are asked to dress appropriately and to respect the silence and recollected atmosphere of the abbey.
- Family photographs without a tripod are allowed, but not during services.
- Guided visits are in French; the abbey suggests its self-guided HistoPad tablet tour, available in 11 languages, for non-French speakers. Check the same page for current visiting hours and to book.

Go early, keep your voice down, and enjoy the view from the road above rather than from the middle of a field.

## Lavender etiquette

This matters more than it might seem. The fields are private farmland, and the crop is someone's income. The Valensole tourism page puts it plainly: [do not enter the fields](https://en.tourisme-alpes-haute-provence.com/grands-espaces-naturels/4860347_curiosite-naturelle-valensole-lavender-and-lavandin/). The regional board asks visitors [not to pick any lavender](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/en/provence/things-to-do/outdoor-activities/5-spots-to-admire-lavender-in-bloom/), out of respect for the growers and for the bees working the flowers.

In practice, that means:

- Photograph from the edge, the verge or a public track. Do not walk down the rows or sit among the plants.
- Never drive or park on field margins or farm tracks; pull over only where it is safe and legal.
- Do not pick stems, however few. Buy bunches, sachets and oil from a farm shop or market instead.
- Watch where you step: the bees are everywhere and entirely focused on the flowers. Keep dogs on a lead, which is required in the Verdon regional park according to the [Valensole page](https://en.tourisme-alpes-haute-provence.com/grands-espaces-naturels/4860347_curiosite-naturelle-valensole-lavender-and-lavandin/).

Plenty of farms and distilleries welcome visitors properly, with shops and demonstrations; the [regional tourism board lists several](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/en/provence/things-to-do/outdoor-activities/5-spots-to-admire-lavender-in-bloom/), including the Lavender Museum at Cabrières-d'Avignon. That is where to get close to the plant.

## Hilltop villages

Between the fields, the villages are the other half of the story. Lavender season coincides with long evenings, and there is no better way to spend one than wandering a perched village as the stone cools.

- **Gordes** clings to the side of a plateau in the Luberon regional nature park, and the regional board describes it as [an acropolis looking out over the valley](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/fiche-circuit/les-plus-beaux-villages/). Beautiful, and very busy by midday.
- **Ménerbes**, a short drive away, is quieter, all old stone and a sense of slow living; the same circuit notes its truffle market.
- **Roussillon** is built on ochre, and the [Sentier des Ocres](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/en/offers/sentier-des-ocres-roussillon-en-3261133/) leads through the old quarries in red, orange and yellow. There are two routes, of around 30 and 50 minutes, with 350 steps, so it is not suitable for pushchairs or reduced mobility, and opening is subject to the weather.
- **Moustiers-Sainte-Marie**, east of Valensole, is [backed against a cliff at the entrance to the Verdon gorges](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/fiche-circuit/les-plus-beaux-villages/) and known for its faience, with a museum and potters' shops. It pairs naturally with a day on the plateau.

## Markets

A Provençal market in high summer is a joy if you get there early: melons and apricots, goat's cheeses, olives, honey, lavender in every form, and the clatter of stalls going up while it is still cool. By late morning it is hot and packed, so treat markets as breakfast outings.

The Pays d'Apt Luberon tourist office lists the [weekly market days](https://en.luberon-apt.fr/node/710), including:

- **Apt**: Saturday morning, the big one, plus a farmers' market on Tuesday morning.
- **Gordes**: Tuesday morning.
- **Lourmarin** and **Bonnieux**: Friday morning.

Confirm the day before you go, as summer arrangements can change.

## Heat and fire

Lavender season is also the hottest stretch of the Provençal year, and both heat and wildfire risk shape what you can do.

Plan your days around the temperature: fields and villages early and late, a long lunch and a shuttered siesta in between. Carry more water than you think you need, and never leave anyone or anything in a parked car. Check the [Météo-France vigilance map](https://vigilance.meteofrance.fr/fr), which issues orange and red heatwave warnings, before you set out each day.

Many of the hills and forests you'll want to walk in can be closed on high-risk days. In the Vaucluse, the state services publish a [daily map of which forest massifs are open or closed](https://www.risque-prevention-incendie.fr/vaucluse/), updated each evening at 18:00 for the following day. Check it the night before any walk, and if a massif is closed, respect it. Never light a fire or drop a cigarette end anywhere near dry vegetation.

## Where to stay

Where you sleep makes or breaks a lavender trip, because the fields are spread out and the roads between them are slow.

- **The Luberon (around Gordes, Bonnieux, Ménerbes or Apt)** is our first choice for a week. It puts Sénanque, the ochre villages and the best markets within easy reach, and Sault is a scenic drive away.
- **Sault or the villages of its plateau** suit anyone timing their trip for July. Staying here means you can be in the fields at dawn, and the altitude makes nights more comfortable.
- **Valensole, Riez or Moustiers-Sainte-Marie** are the logical base for late June, close to the plateau's earliest flowering and to the Verdon gorges.
- **Two bases are better than one** for a week or more: a few nights near Valensole followed by a few in the Luberon or around Sault lets you follow the flowering rather than chase it.

Book early for July, and favour somewhere with shade, outdoor space and, ideally, a pool. In this heat, they are not luxuries.

## Before you go

- Check the flowering updates from the [Valensole](https://en.tourisme-alpes-haute-provence.com/grands-espaces-naturels/4860347_curiosite-naturelle-valensole-lavender-and-lavandin/) and [regional](https://provence-alpes-cotedazur.com/en/provence/things-to-do/outdoor-activities/5-spots-to-admire-lavender-in-bloom/) tourism pages a week or two before travelling.
- Book a car: the fields and hill villages are poorly served by public transport. Our guide to [getting around France](/posts/getting-around-france-trains-driving/) covers driving rules and picking up a hire car from the station.
- Check visiting hours and book ahead for [Sénanque](https://www.senanque.fr/visite-de-labbaye/).
- Pack a sun hat, high-factor sun cream, a refillable water bottle and modest clothing for the abbey.
- Check the [heat vigilance](https://vigilance.meteofrance.fr/fr) and [forest access map](https://www.risque-prevention-incendie.fr/vaucluse/) every evening.
- Stay out of the fields, pick nothing, and buy your lavender from the people who grow it.
