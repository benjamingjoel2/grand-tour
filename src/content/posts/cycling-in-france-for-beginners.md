The first hour is always the same. A slightly wobbly start, a hired bike that feels unfamiliar, a check of the panniers, and then the path opens up beside the water and the town falls away behind you. Poplars tick past. A heron lifts off the bank. A lock-keeper's cottage, shutters painted the colour of the sky, slides by on the far side of the canal. By lunchtime you have stopped thinking about the bike at all, and you are simply moving through France at the speed it was meant to be seen.

You do not need to be fit, brave or kitted out in Lycra to have this experience. France has a network of long-distance cycle routes, many of them flat, signed and largely away from traffic, that are made for people who have not ridden seriously in years. This guide covers the routes we would point a beginner towards, the rules of the road you genuinely need to know, and how to get a bike on a train.

## Why France works for first-timers

The secret is water. Rivers and canals do not climb hills, so the routes that follow them are gentle, and the old towpaths and riverside levees make natural cycle tracks. Add a culture where a long lunch is a perfectly reasonable part of a day's ride, villages at frequent intervals, and a national label for bike-friendly businesses, and you have about the most forgiving place in Europe to start.

That label is **Accueil Vélo**. [La Loire à Vélo explains](https://www.loireavelo.fr/la-marque-accueil-velo/) that it is a national quality mark for tourism businesses within 5 km of a cycle route, and that accommodation carrying it must provide things such as a secure bike shelter and a repair kit. Look for the logo when you book; it takes most of the worry out of where to leave your bike overnight.

[France Vélo Tourisme](https://www.francevelotourisme.com/itineraire/la-loire-a-velo), the national cycle tourism site, grades routes by level. Its "Je débute" (I'm a beginner) label is the one to look for.

## La Loire à Vélo: the obvious first ride

If you only ever do one French cycle trip, make it this one. [France Vélo Tourisme](https://www.francevelotourisme.com/itineraire/la-loire-a-velo) rates La Loire à Vélo as suitable for beginners and describes it as fully signed in both directions, from Nevers to Saint-Brevin-les-Pins on the Atlantic coast, with signs carrying the route's logo and the EuroVelo 6 identifier.

What makes it special is the rhythm: river, vineyard, village, château, river again. You ride along embankments with the wide, braided Loire on one side, past sandbanks and willow islands, and roll into towns like Blois, Amboise, Saumur and Tours in time for a late lunch.

For a first trip, pick one of the sections France Vélo Tourisme flags as mostly dedicated cycle path and good for a first family outing. Our favourites for beginners:

- **Montlouis-sur-Loire – Tours – Villandry**, which pairs naturally with the châteaux in our [Loire Valley guide](/posts/loire-valley-chateaux/).
- **Candé-sur-Beuvron – Chaumont-sur-Loire**, short and pretty.
- **La Daguenière – Bouchemaine – Angers**, for the western Loire and its tufa-stone villages.

Two or three days on one of these, with luggage-light riding between a pair of bases, is a perfect introduction.

## Burgundy's canals: towpaths and vineyards

Burgundy is the route for people who love the idea of cycling slowly between wine villages. [France Vélo Tourisme's Tour de Bourgogne à vélo](https://www.francevelotourisme.com/itineraire/le-tour-de-bourgogne-a-velo) is a loop through canals and vineyards, following towpaths and greenways laid on old railway lines, and it is graded for beginners on large parts of its length.

The site singles out two canal sections as good for families:

- **The Canal de Bourgogne from Migennes to Dijon**, a long, green, lock-studded towpath.
- **The Canal du Nivernais from Decize to Auxerre**, quieter and more rural.

Riding a towpath is almost meditative. There is the hum of tyres on gravel, the click of a lock gate somewhere ahead, and long avenues of plane trees throwing stripes of shade across the path. Pair a couple of days here with our [wine lover's week in Burgundy](/posts/burgundy-wine-week/), and save the tasting for after you have stopped riding for the day.

## La Vélodyssée: the Atlantic coast

[La Vélodyssée](https://www.francevelotourisme.com/itineraire/la-velodyssee) runs from Roscoff in Brittany to Hendaye near the Spanish border. France Vélo Tourisme grades it for beginners, calls it one of the safest routes in France, and says more than 80% of it runs on dedicated cycle routes. Expect canal towpaths in Brittany, salt marshes and pine forests further south, and the smell of the sea for much of the way.

For a first taste, France Vélo Tourisme describes the section from **Fromentine to Les Sables-d'Olonne** as the safest stretch of the whole route. It is beach-hopping country: ride for an hour, swim, ride again.

## ViaRhôna: from the Alps to the Mediterranean

[ViaRhôna](https://www.francevelotourisme.com/itineraire/viarhona) follows the Rhône from Lake Geneva to the Mediterranean, and France Vélo Tourisme describes its profile as flat for most of the way. There are a few things to know before you choose a section, though:

- The upper section, from Lake Geneva to Seyssel, is hillier and recommended for riders who are used to cycling.
- From Seyssel to the sea, the route is described as broadly flat and accessible to beginners and families.
- The site warns that the exit from Lyon is tricky and recommends taking the train on the provisional Lyon–Givors stretch.
- **Vienne to Valence** is singled out as the easiest and best-protected section for families new to cycling.

The southern end slides into Provence, through vineyards and past old river towns; our [Provence in lavender season](/posts/provence-lavender-season/) post has ideas for the countryside either side.

## The rules of the road

French road rules apply to cyclists just as they do to drivers. The French road safety authority, Sécurité Routière, sets them out clearly, and these are the ones that matter most for visitors.

**Equipment.** According to [Sécurité Routière's equipment page](https://www.securite-routiere.gouv.fr/reglementation-liee-aux-modes-de-deplacements/velo/equipements-obligatoires-velo), your bike must have two brakes, front and rear; front and rear lights; reflectors (red at the back, white or yellow at the front, orange on the sides and pedals); and a bell or horn that can be heard at least 50 metres away. Any reputable hire bike will have these, but check before you set off.

**Helmets and hi-vis.** The same page says helmets are compulsory, and must be fastened, for riders and passengers under 12. Adults are not obliged to wear one, though Sécurité Routière strongly makes the safety case for them, and so do we. A certified reflective vest is compulsory for every cyclist and passenger riding outside built-up areas at night or when visibility is poor.

**On the road.** [Sécurité Routière's traffic rules page](https://www.securite-routiere.gouv.fr/reglementation-liee-aux-modes-de-deplacements/velo/regles-de-circulation-pour-les-cyclistes) is worth reading in full. The essentials:

- Ride on the right-hand side of the road where there is no cycle lane.
- Cycle lanes are compulsory where they are marked by a round sign, and advisory where the sign is square.
- In a group, ride two abreast or in single file, and drop to single file at night, when a vehicle is overtaking, or when the road is narrow. Groups of more than ten should split.
- Do not ride on pavements; only children under eight may do so, at a sensible speed.
- Earphones, earbuds and headphones are banned while riding, as is holding a phone.
- Riding with a breath-alcohol level of 0.25 mg/l or more is illegal. Our advice is simpler: save the wine for the evening.

## Bikes on trains

Trains are a beginner's best friend: ride one way, take the train back, or skip a dull or busy section entirely. But the rules differ by train type, so check before you travel.

[SNCF Connect's bike page](https://www.sncf-connect.com/aide/transport-de-votre-velo) explains that on TGV INOUI and INTERCITÉS, some trains have bookable bike spaces, and that the reservation must be made at the same time as you buy your ticket. A bike that is dismantled and packed in a labelled cover is always accepted. On TER regional trains, rules depend on the region, and many bike spaces cannot be booked through SNCF Connect at all.

On the Loire specifically, [La Loire à Vélo's train page](https://www.loireavelo.fr/se-deplacer-sur-litineraire/ter-et-cars-regionaux/) advises avoiding weekday rush hours, and notes that some lines require a bike reservation at weekends or in summer. France Vélo Tourisme's Burgundy page also warns that carrying bikes on TER can be difficult at peak times. Our advice: travel mid-morning, mid-week where you can, and have a plan B. For everything else about French rail, see our guide to [getting around France](/posts/getting-around-france-trains-driving/).

## Where to stay

The beginner's trick is to pick two or three bases and ride out-and-back or point-to-point between them, rather than moving every night.

- **Loire:** Tours, Blois or Saumur make lively bases with stations; Amboise and Chaumont-sur-Loire are quieter.
- **Burgundy:** Dijon for city evenings and trains; a canal-side village for the full towpath experience.
- **Atlantic coast:** a seaside town near the Fromentine–Les Sables-d'Olonne stretch, so a swim is never far away.
- **Rhône:** Vienne or Valence for the gentlest section of ViaRhôna.

Wherever you stay, look for the Accueil Vélo logo and ask how bikes are stored overnight.

## Before you go

- **Pick a "Je débute" section** on [France Vélo Tourisme](https://www.francevelotourisme.com/itineraire/la-loire-a-velo) and plan short days. Leave time for lunch, swims and getting lost.
- **Hire locally.** Bike and e-bike rental is widespread on the major routes; book ahead in high season.
- **Check the bike.** Brakes, lights, reflectors and bell are [legal requirements](https://www.securite-routiere.gouv.fr/reglementation-liee-aux-modes-de-deplacements/velo/equipements-obligatoires-velo), not extras.
- **Pack a reflective vest** if there is any chance you will ride outside towns after dark or in poor light.
- **Leave the earphones in your bag** while riding.
- **Book train bike spaces** when you buy the ticket, using [SNCF Connect](https://www.sncf-connect.com/aide/transport-de-votre-velo).
- **Consider an e-bike.** It flattens headwinds and the odd climb, and makes longer days far more comfortable for nervous riders.
