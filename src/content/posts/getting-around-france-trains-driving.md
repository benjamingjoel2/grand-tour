There is a particular pleasure in a French railway station on a summer morning: the hum of the departures board, the smell of coffee and warm pastry, the high-speed train sliding in sleek and silent, and the knowledge that in a few hours you will step out somewhere with a completely different light. There is another pleasure, just as real, in a hire car on an empty departmental road, plane trees flickering past, with nothing between you and the next village but a field of sunflowers.

France is one of the easiest countries in Europe to explore without a plan for every minute, as long as you understand how the trains work and what the road rules expect of you. This guide covers both.

## The trains: who runs what

Most domestic trains are booked through SNCF Connect, and the brand names can be confusing at first. Here is the short version.

### TGV INOUI

TGV INOUI is SNCF's classic high-speed service, the train you picture when someone says "TGV". France's tourism board notes that [TGVs can exceed 250 km/h](https://www.france.fr/en/article/Looking-to-travel-around-France-by-train/), linking Paris to the big regional cities and beyond. It is the comfortable, flexible choice for longer journeys.

### OUIGO

OUIGO is SNCF's low-cost brand. SNCF Connect's [guide to travelling with OUIGO](https://www.sncf-connect.com/en-en/help/travelling-ouigo) describes two products: OUIGO Grande Vitesse, which runs on the high-speed network, and OUIGO Train Classique, which uses conventional lines. The savings come with trade-offs that catch out first-timers:

- **Luggage is limited.** The basic fare includes a handbag-sized item and one cabin-size bag; extra or oversized luggage is a paid option.
- **Arrive early.** SNCF Connect says to be on the platform 30 minutes before departure, and that access to the train closes 5 minutes before it leaves.
- **No buffet car**, so bring your own picnic.
- **Tickets cannot be cancelled or refunded**, only exchanged for a fee.
- **Check the station.** From Paris, depending on the destination, OUIGO may leave from Montparnasse, Gare de l'Est or Gare de Lyon, or from Marne-la-Vallée, Massy or Charles de Gaulle airport.

Our advice: OUIGO is excellent value for travellers with light bags and fixed plans. If you are carrying a big suitcase or might change your mind, compare the total cost with TGV INOUI before you book.

### TER regional trains

TER trains are the regional and local services, run region by region. France.fr describes them as [generally slower, covering regional and local routes](https://www.france.fr/en/article/Looking-to-travel-around-France-by-train/). They are what you will use to reach smaller towns, wine villages and coastal resorts once the TGV has dropped you in a regional hub. They also tend to be the most relaxed way to travel with a bike: France.fr says bikes travel free on TER in designated areas when space allows, though regional rules may apply. For more on that, see our [cycling in France for beginners](/posts/cycling-in-france-for-beginners/).

### Intercités

Intercités trains fill the gap between TGV and TER on longer conventional routes. Some require a reservation and some do not. They also run France's domestic night trains: France.fr lists [Intercités sleeper services from Paris-Austerlitz](https://www.france.fr/en/article/Looking-to-travel-around-France-by-train/) to destinations including Nice, Toulouse and Briançon, with a choice of a standard seat, a sleeper cabin or a private compartment. A night train is a lovely way to wake up in the mountains or by the sea without losing a day.

## Booking on SNCF Connect

The official place to book is SNCF Connect, as a website or a free app. A few things worth knowing:

- **When tickets go on sale.** SNCF Connect says that [under normal circumstances you can buy train tickets 4 months in advance](https://www.sncf-connect.com/en-en/help/ticket-sales-opening). OUIGO Train Classique tickets within France go on sale 60 days before departure, and TER sales open 3 to 5 months ahead depending on the region. Around the summer, Christmas and winter holidays, opening dates can shift, and the return half of a trip may open later than the outward one.
- **Fares change.** We won't quote prices; check the current fare on [SNCF Connect](https://www.sncf-connect.com/en-en/help/buy-book-train-ticket-online), where you can also set a low-fare alert for a route.
- **E-tickets are easiest.** SNCF Connect's [e-ticket guide](https://www.sncf-connect.com/en-en/help/e-ticket) explains that e-tickets work across TGV, Intercités and TER journeys in France, you can download the barcode to your phone, and there is no need to validate them on the platform. They are personal and non-transferable, so enter each traveller's real name.
- **If a train doesn't appear**, it may not be on sale yet, it may be full, or engineering works may be under way. SNCF Connect's [troubleshooting page](https://www.sncf-connect.com/en-en/help/train-not-found) suggests trying alternative stations, such as Aix-en-Provence TGV for Marseille or Lyon Saint-Exupéry for Lyon, or splitting a journey into two searches.

### What about rail passes?

Rail passes can pay off for travellers covering a lot of ground at short notice. The [Interrail France Pass](https://www.interrail.com/en/interrail-passes/one-country-pass/france) covers participating operators within France, but you cannot buy one for your own country of residence, and Interrail warns that most high-speed and night trains need a reservation at extra cost. Do the sums against point-to-point tickets for your actual route before you buy. For a worked example of a rail-only trip, see our [14-day France itinerary by train](/posts/france-14-day-itinerary-by-train/).

## Driving in France

For the Dordogne, rural Provence, Burgundy's back roads or anywhere the best village sits up a hill with no station, a car is hard to beat. Here is what to know.

### Your licence and documents

- **Licence.** If your licence was issued in the EU or EEA, you can use it in France as long as it is valid and you meet the conditions set out on [Service-Public's page for European licences](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F1757). For other licences on a short stay, [Service-Public explains](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F1459) that a licence not written in French must be accompanied by an international driving permit or an official translation, with an exception for British licences. A short stay means under 185 days.
- **UK drivers.** The FCDO's [France travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/france/safety-and-security) says a UK photocard licence can be used in France, but that you may need a UK sticker on your car, and that hire companies often set stricter conditions, such as a higher minimum age or an international driving permit. Check with your hire company before you travel.
- **Vehicle papers.** You must be able to show the vehicle's registration certificate at a roadside check, and [Service-Public notes](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F2794) that a copy is accepted for a short-term hire car.

### The rules of the road

France drives on the right, and the national speed limits for experienced drivers are set out on [Service-Public's speed page](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F19460):

- **Motorways (autoroutes):** 130 km/h, reduced to 110 km/h in rain or other precipitation.
- **Dual carriageways with a central reservation:** 110 km/h.
- **Two-way roads without a central separator:** 80 km/h, although the local road authority can raise this to 90 km/h on some roads, so watch the signs.
- **Built-up areas:** 50 km/h.

Lower limits apply to newly qualified drivers and are often posted locally, so treat the signs as the final word.

On alcohol, [Service-Public's drink-driving page](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F2881) sets the general limit at 0.5 g of alcohol per litre of blood, and 0.2 g/l for probationary drivers. The FCDO points out that this is a third lower than in England and Wales. Our advice is simple: if you are touring wine country, nominate a driver who doesn't taste, or spit.

### What to carry

[Service-Public's equipment page](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F19459) says you must keep a high-visibility vest and a warning triangle in the car. A breathalyser is not compulsory. In certain mountain communes, from 1 November to 31 March, you must fit winter tyres or carry snow chains. If you are heading into the Alps, Pyrenees or Massif Central in winter, ask your hire company exactly what the car is equipped with.

### Crit'Air stickers and low-emission zones

Several French cities have low-emission zones, known as ZFE-m. [Service-Public](https://www.service-public.gouv.fr/particuliers/vosdroits/F33371) explains that a Crit'Air sticker, which classifies vehicles by their pollutant emissions, is compulsory to drive in a ZFE-m, and also during pollution episodes when the authorities bring in traffic restrictions. Owners of foreign-registered vehicles can only apply online, and the government site certificat-air.gouv.fr is the only official issuer. Bison Futé, the national traffic service, [warns drivers to beware of intermediaries and fraudulent sites](https://www.bison-fute.gouv.fr/zones-a-faibles-emissions-mobilite.html) selling the stickers.

The FCDO says vehicles in central Paris, Lyon and Grenoble must display the sticker from 8am to 8pm on weekdays, and that other cities may also restrict access. These rules change, so check the zones on your route on Bison Futé before you set off. If you are hiring a car, ask whether it carries a Crit'Air sticker when you pick it up.

### Tolls

Expect to pay tolls (péages) on many autoroutes, so factor them into your budget and check the cost of your route before you set off. Motorway operators such as [VINCI Autoroutes](https://www.vinci-autoroutes.com/fr) offer route planning and electronic toll tags (télépéage). If you are not in a hurry, the toll-free national and departmental roads are slower but often far prettier, and they take you through the villages rather than past them.

### Traffic and timing

Summer weekends can be very busy on the main routes south. [Bison Futé](https://www.bison-fute.gouv.fr/index,langen.html) publishes traffic forecasts by date, a calendar of the busiest days and real-time conditions. We check it before any long drive and, where we can, avoid setting off on the days it flags as busiest.

## Train or car: our verdict

Use the train between regions and the car within them. A TGV will get you from Paris to Provence, the Alps or Bordeaux faster and more calmly than any motorway, and a hire car picked up at a regional station opens up the countryside. If your trip is mostly cities and big towns, you may not need a car at all.

## Before you go

- Download the SNCF Connect app and check when tickets for your dates go on sale.
- If you book OUIGO, check the departure station and the luggage rules carefully.
- Check your licence requirements on Service-Public and with your hire company.
- Make sure the car has a high-visibility vest and warning triangle, and winter equipment if you are heading into the mountains between November and March.
- Check Bison Futé for low-emission zones and traffic forecasts on your route.
- Only buy a Crit'Air sticker from certificat-air.gouv.fr.
- Read the FCDO's [France travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/france/safety-and-security) for the latest on strikes and road safety.
