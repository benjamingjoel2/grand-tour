The first sight of Chambord comes through the trees. The road runs dead straight through a forest of oak, the canopy closes overhead, and then it opens onto a lawn and a vision that seems far too large and far too intricate to be real: a pale stone mass topped with a skyline of turrets, chimneys, lanterns and dormers, like a small town that has climbed onto the roof. Early in the morning, with mist still lying over the moat and only a handful of cars in the car park, it is one of the great moments of travel in France.

The trouble with the Loire is that it offers dozens of moments like that, and the temptation is to try to collect them all. Don't. Three châteaux in a day is a blur of tapestries and ticket queues; one or two, with a long lunch and a river walk in between, is a holiday. Here are the ones we think are genuinely worth your time, and how to fit them together.

## Why the Loire, and how to approach it

The stretch of river between [Sully-sur-Loire and Chalonnes-sur-Loire has been on the UNESCO World Heritage List since 30 November 2000](https://www.valdeloire.org/en/), recognised as an exceptional cultural landscape along a river rather than for any single building. That is worth bearing in mind. The châteaux are the headline, but the pleasure is also in the white tufa towns, the vineyards, the poplars and sandbanks and the slow, wide, unpredictable river itself.

Our advice for a first visit:

- **Choose four to six châteaux** for a week, not twelve.
- **Mix the grand with the intimate.** Follow a day at Chambord with a small, lived-in house or a garden.
- **Go early.** The big names are at their best in the first hour after opening, before the coaches.
- **Leave time for the river.** Walk or cycle a stretch of towpath, sit on a terrace in Amboise or Saumur, taste some Vouvray or Chinon.

## Chambord: the showstopper

If you see only one château, make it this one. Chambord was begun for François I: according to the estate's own history, [construction officially started on 6 September 1519](https://www.chambord.org/en/history/). It was conceived as a hunting lodge built to impress, and the whole thing feels like a statement of power in stone.

At its heart is the famous double-helix staircase, two spirals winding around each other so that people going up and down never meet. France's tourism agency notes that the design is [credited to Leonardo da Vinci](https://www.france.fr/en/article/all-you-need-to-know-about-the-loire-valley-chateaux-in-5-minutes/), and whether or not he drew it, climbing it is irresistible. Take it all the way up to the roof, where you walk among the chimneys and lanterns with views out over the forest.

Don't rush away afterwards. The château sits within the [Domaine national de Chambord](https://www.chambord.org/en/), which includes formal gardens, a village and walking trails through a forest the estate describes as part of Europe's largest enclosed nature reserve. Hours change with the seasons, so check the official site before you set out, and pack a picnic.

## Chenonceau: the château on the river

Chenonceau is the other essential, and the opposite of Chambord in mood: elegant, intimate and reflected in water. Its gallery stretches across the river Cher, and France's tourism agency lists [the gallery on the River Cher among the rooms you pass through](https://www.france.fr/en/article/chateau-chenonceau/), along with kitchens built into the bridge piers. The château's own site credits [Thomas Bohier and Catherine Briçonnet with its current form, built between 1513 and 1517](https://www.chenonceau.com/en/chateau/), and stresses the long line of women who built, restored and saved it; it is often called the "Château des Dames", and Diane de Poitiers and Catherine de' Medici both left their mark.

The gardens bear their names too. The [France.fr gardens guide](https://www.france.fr/en/article/in-the-gardens-of-the-loire-valley-chateaux/) describes Diane de Poitiers' garden as rigorously geometric and Catherine de' Medici's as more intimate, with roses, lavender and fountains.

Chenonceau is [open every day of the year](https://www.chenonceau.com/en/practical-information/opening-times/), with hours that vary by season, and it is extremely popular. Be at the gate at opening time, see the château first while it is calm, and save the gardens and the riverbank for later.

## Villandry and Azay-le-Rideau: gardens and reflections

West of Tours, two châteaux a short drive apart make a perfect, gentle day.

**Villandry** is all about its gardens. The château's site explains that they were [redesigned by Joachim Carvallo in the early 20th century](https://www.chateauvillandry.fr/en/), drawing on Renaissance, French formal and landscape-park traditions, and that the [gardens are open every day of the year except 25 December](https://www.chateauvillandry.fr/en/). The famous ornamental kitchen garden, laid out on terraces and, as France.fr puts it, [checkered like embroidery](https://www.france.fr/en/article/in-the-gardens-of-the-loire-valley-chateaux/), is the highlight: cabbages and leeks planted with the precision of a parterre. Climb to the upper terraces for the full pattern.

**Azay-le-Rideau** is a jewel of the French Renaissance built under François I, according to its [official site](https://www.azay-le-rideau.fr/en), and is [built on an island between two arms of the river](https://www.france.fr/en/article/in-the-gardens-of-the-loire-valley-chateaux/), with water mirrors in its landscaped park. On a still morning, its reflection is the reason to come. It is smaller and calmer than the big names, which is precisely its appeal.

## Amboise and Clos Lucé: Leonardo's last home

Amboise is the most rewarding town on the river for an overnight stay, and it gives you two visits within a short walk.

The [Château royal d'Amboise](https://www.chateau-amboise.com/en) rises above the town and the Loire, with what it calls the most beautiful panorama of the Loire Valley from its terraces. Leonardo da Vinci is buried in the château's chapel.

The [Château du Clos Lucé](https://vinci-closluce.com/en), Leonardo's last home, is around 400 metres away. Inside you see the rooms where he lived and worked; outside, a park works as an open-air museum, with more than 40 reconstructed machines based on his designs. Children love it, and so, frankly, do most adults.

## Cheverny and Blois: lived-in and royal

**Cheverny** feels different from the state-owned châteaux because it is still a home. Its owners say it has been [in the same family for over six centuries](https://www.chateau-cheverny.fr/en/), and it is known for its richly furnished interiors and themed gardens. Tintin fans will find another reason to go: the château was the model for Captain Haddock's Marlinspike Hall (Moulinsart in French), and [France.fr notes the exhibition run with the Hergé Foundation](https://www.france.fr/en/article/all-you-need-to-know-about-the-loire-valley-chateaux-in-5-minutes/).

**Blois** is a royal château in the middle of a lively town, with wings from different eras around a single courtyard, so you read French architectural history just by turning around. On summer evenings the courtyard hosts a sound-and-light show; the château's site says the [next season begins on 27 March 2027](https://www.chateaudeblois.fr/). Blois is also a practical stop, with shops, restaurants and a station.

## A few we'd skip on a first visit

This is not a slight on anyone, simply a matter of time. On a first trip we'd rather do the places above slowly than add more. With extra days, Chaumont-sur-Loire is worth adding for its [annual international garden festival](https://www.france.fr/en/article/in-the-gardens-of-the-loire-valley-chateaux/), and the western end of the valley, around Chinon, Saumur and Fontevraud, rewards a second trip.

## Getting around

The big châteaux are spread across a wide area, and many are outside town centres, so a car gives the most freedom. Our guide to [getting around France](/posts/getting-around-france-trains-driving/) covers driving and picking up a hire car at a station.

The Loire is also one of the easiest places in France to explore by bike. [La Loire à Vélo](https://www.loireavelo.fr/) is a signposted cycle route of around 900 km from Cuffy, near Nevers, to Saint-Brevin-les-Pins on the Atlantic, and its site lists regional trains fitted out to carry bikes. Riding between Amboise and Chenonceau, or around Villandry, with a château as your lunch stop, is a lovely way to slow down. If you are new to it, read [cycling in France for beginners](/posts/cycling-in-france-for-beginners/) first.

## Where to stay

Pick one or two bases and make day trips, rather than packing every morning.

- **Amboise** is our first choice: a handsome riverside town with two châteaux on foot, and Chenonceau, Chambord and Blois all within reach.
- **Blois** is ideal for the eastern châteaux, Chambord and Cheverny especially, and has a good range of places to eat.
- **Tours** is the practical hub, a lively city with fast train connections, well placed for Villandry and Azay-le-Rideau.
- **Chinon or Saumur** suit a second base in the west, among vineyards and tufa cliffs, if you want to add Azay-le-Rideau, Fontevraud and some wine tasting.
- **Village stays** around Chenonceaux, Azay-le-Rideau or the vineyards of Vouvray trade convenience for quiet. You'll need a car or bike.

For a week, three or four nights in Amboise followed by three in Chinon or Saumur works beautifully.

## Before you go

- Check each château's official site for current opening hours, which change by season, and buy tickets online for Chambord and Chenonceau in high season.
- Plan no more than two châteaux a day, and put the busiest one first thing in the morning.
- Pack comfortable shoes: spiral staircases, gravel paths and garden terraces add up.
- Bring a picnic for Chambord's park and a light layer for cool château interiors, even in summer.
- If you plan to cycle, check the [Loire à Vélo](https://www.loireavelo.fr/) site for route sections and bike-friendly trains.
- Leave an afternoon free for the river itself.
