Early morning on the Dordogne, and the river is the colour of weak tea, sliding past shingle banks with barely a ripple. Mist lifts off the water in slow curls. Above you, the limestone glows the colour of honey as the sun reaches it, and somewhere up there, sitting on the cliff as if it grew out of the rock, is a castle. By ten the first canoes will be out, by eleven the market stalls in Sarlat will be loud with walnut sellers and cheesemongers, and by the afternoon you could be standing in the half-dark of a cave, looking at a bison painted by someone who lived many thousands of years before the castle was even an idea.

That is the Dordogne in one day, and it is why people come back to it for decades. This is a region that rewards a slow pace: a week here, based in one or two places, beats a frantic tour every time. Here is how we would put it together.

## The caves: start with Lascaux

The story everyone knows is true. On 12 September 1940, four teenagers stumbled on a cave near Montignac and found its walls covered in paintings of horses, aurochs and deer. Visitors flocked in after the war, and their breath and the lights did real damage: the [official Lascaux history page](https://lascaux.fr/en/history-of-lascaux/) explains that the cave was closed to the public in 1963 to protect the paintings. A partial replica, Lascaux II, followed in 1983, and in 2016 Lascaux IV opened as a full copy of the entire cave.

You cannot visit the original. The [Lascaux FAQ](https://lascaux.fr/en/questions/) is blunt about it, and that is as it should be. What you can do is visit [Lascaux IV, the International Centre for Cave Art](https://www.lascaux.fr/en) on the edge of Montignac, and our advice is not to be sniffy about it being a replica. The copy is extraordinarily faithful, the lighting and the cool, hushed atmosphere are carefully done, and the interpretation afterwards helps you understand what you have just seen. Allow time: the FAQ says a visit takes an average of 1 to 1.5 hours depending on the options you choose.

Practical points from the official site:

- The centre recommends booking in advance to guarantee your place, and tickets are time-stamped rather than valid all day.
- The [opening hours page](https://lascaux.fr/en/opening-hours-and-prices/) asks you to arrive 20 minutes before your slot, and last admission is two hours before the site closes. Hours vary through the year, so check the page for your dates, along with the current ticket price.
- Pets are not allowed, except guide dogs.

## Beyond Lascaux: the real painted caves of the Vézère

If Lascaux IV is the overture, the original decorated caves around Les Eyzies are the main event. A handful are still open to small groups, and seeing genuine prehistoric paintings by lamplight is one of the most moving things you can do in France.

The state-run sites around Les Eyzies, including the Grotte de Font-de-Gaume and the Grotte des Combarelles, are managed by Les monuments nationaux. Their [practical information page](https://www.sites-les-eyzies.fr/en/visit/practical-information) is clear on the rules:

- The number of visitors admitted each day is limited, and guided tours must be reserved online.
- Visits are suited to children aged seven and over.
- Time inside Font-de-Gaume is limited to 30 minutes for conservation reasons; allow about an hour on site per monument.
- The Font-de-Gaume reception is closed on Saturdays.

Book these as soon as your dates are fixed. These are the visits that sell out, and they are the ones you will remember.

Nearby, the [Musée national de Préhistoire](https://musee-prehistoire-eyzies.fr/) in Les Eyzies is the perfect rainy-morning companion, with collections covering tools, ornaments, portable art and burials. Note that it closes on Tuesdays outside July and August.

North of the Vézère, the [Grotte de Rouffignac](https://www.grottederouffignac.fr/) is a different experience again: a huge cave you tour by small electric train, with more than 250 animal figures on its walls. The site recommends reserving ahead, and at the time of writing it shows the cave closed until 28 March 2027, so plan Rouffignac for spring onwards.

## The castles: Beynac and Castelnaud

The stretch of the Dordogne between Beynac and Castelnaud is where the castles come thickest. For centuries this river was a frontier, and the fortresses face each other across the water like chess pieces left mid-game.

**Beynac.** The village of Beynac-et-Cazenac tumbles down the slope beneath its [listed historic castle](https://en.sarlat-tourisme.com/discover-sarlat-and-the-perigord/the-most-beautiful-villages-of-france/), which crowns the cliff above the river. Walk up through the steep lanes rather than driving: the climb is the best part, with the roofs of lauze stone and the river opening up below you at every turn. Go early or late in summer, when the light is soft and the coaches have gone.

**Castelnaud.** Across the water, the [Château de Castelnaud](https://castelnaud.com/?lang=en) is a 13th-century fortress that now houses the Museum of Medieval Warfare, with weapons, armour and life-size siege machines. Children love it, and so will most adults; the trebuchet demonstrations at set times are the highlight. The site says it sits 150 metres above the Dordogne valley, and the view from the ramparts is one of the great panoramas of the region. It is open every day of the year and no reservation is needed; check the site for the day's hours and current admission price.

Between the two, the cliff villages are just as compelling. The tourist office describes [La Roque-Gageac](https://en.sarlat-tourisme.com/discover-sarlat-and-the-perigord/the-most-beautiful-villages-of-france/) as built along an impressively steep cliffside, and Domme as a golden-stone bastide perched on the cliff with ramparts and a panoramic view over the valley. We would happily spend half a day in each. If you love castles, pair this trip with our guide to [the Loire Valley châteaux worth your time](/posts/loire-valley-chateaux/) for a very different style of grandeur.

## Sarlat and the markets

Sarlat is the Dordogne's market town par excellence, and the market is the reason to set your alarm. The [Sarlat tourist office](https://en.sarlat-tourisme.com/discover-sarlat-and-the-perigord/a-gourmet-escapade-through-the-sarlat-market/) says to head into the medieval town on Wednesday morning or all day on Saturday. Depending on the season, you will find foie gras, ceps, preserved duck, truffles, walnuts, honey and wine, all piled up among golden stone façades that look almost too perfect to be real.

A few more things worth knowing:

- The covered market sits behind the immense door of the former Sainte-Marie church in the historic centre: cheeses, fruit, vegetables and cakes under a soaring roof.
- An organic market runs all year at Place de la Poste on late Thursday afternoons.
- In winter, the same page notes that locals gather at the fattened duck and goose markets on Saturday mornings, and at the truffle markets.
- The [Truffle Fête of Sarlat](https://en.sarlat-tourisme.com/discover-sarlat-and-the-perigord/the-great-events-of-sarlat/the-truffle-fete-of-sarlat/) takes place in mid-January each year, with a truffle market, workshops and truffle-hunting demonstrations with trained dogs.

The smaller village markets are often more relaxed. The tourist office's [guide to the markets of the Périgord Noir](https://en.sarlat-tourisme.com/discover-sarlat-and-the-perigord/a-gourmet-escapade-through-the-sarlat-market/markets-of-the-perigord-noir/) lists dozens, including a seasonal market at Beynac from mid-June to mid-September, and points out that the weekly markets at Beynac, Marquay and Salignac carry the official "Marchés des Producteurs de Pays" label, meaning farmers selling their own produce. In high summer, look out for the night markets, held from the end of June to the beginning of September: producers cook on the spot, there is music, and everyone sits down together at long communal tables. It is the most sociable evening you will have in the region.

Our advice: bring a basket and plan a picnic rather than a restaurant lunch on market day. Bread, a wedge of local cheese, walnuts, a jar of something duck-based and a bottle from Bergerac, eaten on a riverside beach, is the Dordogne at its best.

## On the river

The castles look best from the water. The [Sarlat tourist office's water activities page](https://en.sarlat-tourisme.com/choose-your-activities/leisure-and-outdoor-activities/around-the-water/) lists hire bases along the edge of the Dordogne offering canoes, kayaks, paddleboards and trips on gabarres, the flat-bottomed wooden boats that once carried cargo down the river. Most people choose a half-day or full-day paddle, and there are little beaches along the banks where you can stop, swim and eat.

The classic way to do it is a one-way paddle: the hire base drives you upstream and you float back down past the villages and castles. It is gentle, the current does a good share of the work, and it is perfectly manageable for families. Wear the buoyancy aid you are given, take sun protection and water, and ask the hire base about current river conditions before you set off. If paddling is not your thing, a gabarre trip gives you the same views from a comfortable seat.

## Where to stay

The Dordogne works best if you pick one or two bases and make day trips. Distances look short on the map, but the roads wind, and summer traffic in the villages can be slow.

- **Sarlat.** The obvious base and a good one: markets on the doorstep, plenty of restaurants, and both the castle stretch of the river and the Vézère caves within easy reach. It is busy in high summer; stay just outside town if you want quiet nights.
- **The Dordogne villages (Beynac, La Roque-Gageac, Domme, Castelnaud).** Wonderful in the evening once day visitors leave, and ideal if canoeing and castles are your priority. Expect steep lanes and limited parking.
- **Les Eyzies and the Vézère valley (including Montignac).** The best choice if caves are the heart of your trip. Les Eyzies is small and atmospheric, and you will be close to Font-de-Gaume, the museum and Lascaux IV.

For a week, we would split it: three or four nights near Sarlat or on the Dordogne, then three nights in the Vézère valley.

## Getting there and around

You really need a car here. Public transport between the villages and caves is limited, and the joy of the region lies in pulling over at a viewpoint, a farm shop or an unplanned market. If you are arriving by train or wondering about driving rules, our guide to [getting around France by TGV, regional trains and car](/posts/getting-around-france-trains-driving/) covers the basics. For the region's place in a wider trip, see [the first-timer's guide to France beyond Paris](/posts/france-beyond-paris/).

As for timing, spring and early autumn are the sweet spots: warm enough for the river, quiet enough for the villages, and the cave visits are easier to book. July and August bring the night markets and the full canoe season, but also the crowds.

## Before you go

- **Book the caves first.** Reserve Lascaux IV and the guided visits at Font-de-Gaume and Combarelles online as soon as your dates are fixed, and check each site's opening days.
- **Check seasonal closures.** Some caves close in winter (Rouffignac is closed until late March 2027), and the Musée national de Préhistoire closes on Tuesdays outside July and August.
- **Plan around market days.** Sarlat's big market is on Saturday, with a smaller one on Wednesday morning.
- **Pack for the river.** Swimwear, a dry bag, sun cream and shoes you can get wet.
- **Bring a jumper for the caves,** even in August: they are cool inside.
- **Leave the dog at home for cave days:** Lascaux IV admits only guide dogs.
- **Check current prices** on each official site before you go; we do not list them because they change.
