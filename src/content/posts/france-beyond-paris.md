Picture the moment the train pulls out of Paris and the suburbs fall away. Within minutes the view opens into wide, pale fields, then a church spire, then a village of shuttered houses that seems to have no interest in being looked at. Somewhere ahead is a market square where the morning's cheeses are already sweating gently under striped awnings, a river with a château reflected in it, a hillside of vines combed into neat green rows. This is the France most first-time visitors are really dreaming of, and it starts the moment you leave the capital.

The trouble is choosing. France is big, varied and generous, and every region has a loyal fan club insisting theirs is the one. This guide is our honest introduction: what each of the main regions does best, who it suits, and how to put together a first trip that feels unhurried rather than ticked off.

## First, decide what kind of trip you want

Before you look at a map, ask yourself three questions. Your answers will narrow the field faster than any list of highlights.

- **What do you want your days to feel like?** Châteaux and gardens, wine and long lunches, beaches, mountains, prehistoric caves, war history, walking, cycling? Most regions do two or three of these brilliantly; none does all of them.
- **Train or car?** Some regions reward a car with back roads and hilltop villages; others work beautifully on rails. Our guide to [getting around France by TGV, regional trains and car](/posts/getting-around-france-trains-driving/) explains the trade-offs.
- **How long have you got?** On a first trip we would rather you see two regions properly than five from a motorway. A week suits one region; two weeks comfortably covers two, or a rail loop like our [14-day France itinerary by train](/posts/france-14-day-itinerary-by-train/).

## The regions at a glance

### The Loire Valley: châteaux and gentle rivers

If you have only ever seen French châteaux in pictures, start here. France's tourism board calls the valley a crucible of the French Renaissance, with châteaux "clinging to the riverbanks" and [Chambord and its neighbours](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/loire-valley) at the heart of it all. The landscape is soft and easy: wide, sandy-banked rivers, poplars, orchards and small towns of white tufa stone. It is wonderfully flat for cycling, and close enough to Paris to feel like an easy first step. Read our pick of [the Loire Valley châteaux worth your time](/posts/loire-valley-chateaux/).

**Best for:** first-timers, families, history lovers, gentle cyclists.

### Normandy and Brittany: coast, history and cider country

Normandy is a place of big skies and long beaches, some of which are [memorials to the events of the Second World War](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/normandy). Inland, the countryside is all hedgerows, orchards and cheese; on the coast you will find chalk cliffs and the seaside towns the Impressionists painted. Then there is Mont-Saint-Michel, which France.fr calls the region's number one attraction. Our post on [the D-Day beaches and Mont-Saint-Michel](/posts/normandy-d-day-beaches-mont-saint-michel/) covers both. Brittany, next door, is wilder, with granite coves and a strong Celtic identity.

**Best for:** history, coastal walks, food lovers who like butter, cream and apples.

### Burgundy: wine, abbeys and slow lunches

Burgundy is a gift to anyone who cares about what is in their glass. The region is known for [Pinot Noir and Chardonnay](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/burgundy), and its vineyard plots, the climats, are recognised by UNESCO. Dijon is the regional capital, Beaune its wine town, and France.fr notes that high-speed TGV trains link Paris to both. Beyond the vines lie canals, Romanesque churches and quiet farmland. See our [wine lover's week in Burgundy](/posts/burgundy-wine-week/).

**Best for:** wine and food, cyclists, travellers who like a slower pace.

### The Dordogne and Bordeaux: castles, caves and markets

The Dordogne is the one we find hardest to leave. Honey-coloured villages tumble down to the river, castles glare at each other from opposing cliffs, and the Vézère valley is, in France.fr's words, considered [the cradle of human presence in Europe](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/dordogne-valley). Lascaux IV, a life-size replica of the original cave, lets you see the famous paintings, and in winter the region's black truffles are sold at markets from December to February. Read more in [the Dordogne: castles, caves and markets](/posts/dordogne-castles-caves-markets/).

To the west, [Bordeaux](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/bordeaux) is a handsome UNESCO World Heritage city on the Garonne, with famous vineyards such as Saint-Émilion and the Médoc close by and the Atlantic beaches not far beyond.

**Best for:** food markets, castles, prehistory, wine, families who like canoes.

### Provence: light, lavender and market towns

Provence is the region of the postcards, and it lives up to them: France.fr describes [scents of lavender floating in the air and a chorus of cicadas](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/provence). Expect stone hill villages, olive groves, Roman remains, Cézanne country around Aix-en-Provence and the wild wetlands of the Camargue. It is at its most famous in lavender season, which we cover in [Provence in lavender season](/posts/provence-lavender-season/), but spring and autumn are calmer and kinder to walkers.

**Best for:** villages, markets, painters and photographers, anyone in search of sunshine.

### The Riviera: the Mediterranean in full colour

The [Côte d'Azur](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/cote-dazur) is the blue Mediterranean, golden beaches, perfumed gardens and an artistic heritage that drew many of the great painters of the twentieth century. It is busy and glamorous on the coast, but just behind it the perched villages and the scented hinterland are surprisingly quiet. Nice makes an easy base.

**Best for:** sea and sun, art lovers, a city-and-beach combination.

### The Alps: lakes, peaks and fresh air

The French Alps are a four-season playground, [in the shadow of Mont Blanc](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/alps-mont-blanc): summit walks and swims in clear lakes in summer, ski resorts in winter. If your idea of a holiday involves boots rather than sandals, this is your place.

**Best for:** walkers, skiers, lake swimmers, anyone who wants to feel small in front of a mountain.

### Corsica: the mountain in the sea

[Corsica](https://www.france.fr/en/destination/corsica) feels like a different country: stone villages tucked into mountains, a long, dramatic coastline and the famous GR20 trekking route. Getting there means a ferry from the mainland or a flight, so it suits a second or third trip, or a first trip for keen walkers. Our [Corsica for walkers](/posts/corsica-for-walkers/) post explains the options.

**Best for:** walkers, beach lovers, travellers who want somewhere that feels genuinely different.

## How to choose: some honest pairings

If you are still torn, these are the combinations we suggest most often to first-timers:

- **Châteaux and vineyards:** the Loire Valley plus Burgundy, both within easy reach of Paris.
- **History and coast:** Normandy plus a few days in Brittany or the Loire.
- **Food and countryside:** the Dordogne plus Bordeaux, ideally with a car.
- **Sun and villages:** Provence plus the Riviera, linked by rail or road.
- **Active holidays:** the Alps in summer, or Corsica for walkers.

Our advice is to resist the temptation to add one more region. Every extra move costs you half a day, and the best moments in rural France tend to happen when you have nowhere to be: a long lunch that stretches into the afternoon, a market you stumble upon, a riverside path you follow just to see where it goes.

## Train or car?

France's rail network makes the regions surprisingly reachable. France.fr's guide to [travelling around France by train](https://www.france.fr/en/article/Looking-to-travel-around-France-by-train/) explains that high-speed TGVs connect the big cities while regional TER trains serve local routes, and tickets can be bought in the free SNCF Connect app or online. Trains suit city-hopping and regions such as the Loire, Burgundy and the Riviera.

A car, on the other hand, comes into its own in the Dordogne, rural Provence and anywhere the best villages sit up a hill with no station. Many travellers do both: arrive by train, then pick up a hire car for the countryside. We explain the rules of the road in our [guide to getting around France](/posts/getting-around-france-trains-driving/).

## When to go

Every season has its own France. Spring brings blossom, green vineyards and quiet château gardens; summer is long, hot and lively, with lavender in Provence and the beaches at their busiest; autumn means harvests, golden light and fewer crowds; winter is the time for truffle markets in the Dordogne and snow in the Alps. For a first trip we have a soft spot for late spring and early autumn, when the weather is usually kind, the days are long enough and you can usually find a table without booking weeks ahead.

## Where to stay

The right base matters more than the right hotel. Our general advice:

- **Choose small towns over big cities** for regional trips. A market town with a station or easy road links lets you explore by day and come home to a square full of cafés in the evening. Think Amboise or Saumur in the Loire, Beaune in Burgundy, Sarlat-la-Canéda in the Dordogne, Bayeux in Normandy, or a village near Aix-en-Provence or Avignon in Provence.
- **Stay at least three nights in each place.** One-night stops eat your holiday in packing and checking out.
- **Mix town and country.** A few nights in a lively town, then a few in a farmhouse or village guesthouse (a chambre d'hôtes or gîte), gives you both sides of French life.
- **Travelling by train?** Pick bases near the station so you are not paying for taxis twice a day.

## Before you go

- **Check entry rules.** If you are travelling from the UK, the FCDO's [entry requirements for France](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/france/entry-requirements) explain that your passport must have been issued less than 10 years before the date you arrive and must expire at least 3 months after the date you plan to leave the Schengen area. Visa-free visits are limited to 90 days in any 180-day period, and you may need to register your fingerprints and photo under the EU's Entry/Exit System. Other nationalities should check their own government's advice.
- **Book key trains early.** SNCF Connect says that [under normal circumstances you can buy train tickets 4 months in advance](https://www.sncf-connect.com/en-en/help/ticket-sales-opening), although opening dates vary by type of train and around holiday periods.
- **Check opening seasons** for any château, cave or museum you have your heart set on, on its official website, before you plan your route around it.
- **Plan around market days.** Many towns and villages hold a weekly market; local tourist office websites list the days.
- **Learn a few words of French.** A cheerful "bonjour" on entering a shop or café goes a very long way.
- **Leave space.** Our best advice of all: build in a free day per week for the unplanned.

France beyond Paris rewards curiosity more than planning. Pick one or two regions that match the holiday you actually want, give them time, and let the country do the rest.
