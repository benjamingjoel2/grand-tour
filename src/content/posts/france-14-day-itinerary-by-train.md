The best moment of a French rail journey is rarely the arrival. It is somewhere in the middle: a coffee balanced on the fold-down table, the vineyards of the south-west giving way to pine and then to the pale, sun-bleached stone of Provence, while the person across the aisle sleeps through it all. France is built for this kind of travel. Its cities sit at sensible distances from one another, its stations are mostly in the middle of town, and almost every place on this loop is best explored on foot once you step off the platform.

This itinerary is a two-week circuit from Paris through the Loire Valley, Bordeaux, Provence, the Riviera and the Alps, and back to Paris. It is not a race. We have built in two nights at almost every stop, because the thing that ruins rail trips is not the trains, it is packing a bag every single morning.

## How this route works

The loop runs clockwise: west from Paris to the Loire, south-west to Bordeaux, east across the south to Provence and the coast, then north into the mountains before heading home. You could run it the other way round with no loss.

A word on journey times. We are not going to quote them here, because they change with timetables, engineering works and the type of train you pick, and a figure in a blog post goes stale fast. For every leg, **check SNCF Connect** for current times, connections and fares. Some legs are direct high-speed runs; others involve a change, or a slower regional TER service that is half the point of the trip.

Booking ahead matters on the long-distance legs. [SNCF Connect's own help page](https://www.sncf-connect.com/aide/l-ouverture-des-ventes) says that in normal times you can buy train tickets four months ahead, that TER regional sales open three to five months ahead depending on the region, and that sales for TGV INOUI journeys in France between 7 January and 2 July 2027 open on 12 November 2026. Our advice is to book the long legs (Bordeaux to Provence, and anything in or out of the Riviera) as soon as your dates are fixed, and to leave the short regional hops flexible.

For sibling reading, our guide to [getting around France by TGV, regional train and car](/posts/getting-around-france-trains-driving/) goes deeper on ticket types and stations, and our [first-timer's guide to France beyond Paris](/posts/france-beyond-paris/) helps if you are still deciding where to linger.

## Days 1–3: Paris

Give Paris three nights, not because you will see everything (you will not) but because you need a soft landing. Spend the first day walking: along the Seine, through a market street, into a neighbourhood that is nobody's checklist. Keep one day for a single big museum and one for something unplanned.

Use your last evening to pack light. On this route you will lift your bag onto racks and up station stairs many times, and a small case or a backpack you can carry comfortably will improve every single day that follows.

## Days 4–5: Tours and Amboise (Loire Valley)

The train west out of Paris lands you in the Loire, the softest, most gentle stretch of the whole loop. We'd rather base ourselves in Tours, a lively university city with a good station and an old quarter of timbered houses, than in a château village where evenings can be very quiet. Amboise is a short regional hop away; check TER times on SNCF Connect.

Amboise is the one château to see if you only see one on this trip. The [Château royal d'Amboise](https://www.chateau-amboise.com/) describes itself as the burial place of Leonardo da Vinci, and its terrace offers what the château calls one of the finest panoramas in the Loire Valley. Stand on the ramparts in the late afternoon, with the river broad and slow beneath you and the slate roofs of the town stepping down to the water, and you will understand why kings liked it here.

If the weather is kind, hire a bike for half a day. [France Vélo Tourisme](https://www.francevelotourisme.com/itineraire/la-loire-a-velo) recommends the Montlouis-sur-Loire – Tours – Villandry stretch of La Loire à Vélo as a mostly traffic-free section suitable for a first family outing, which makes it ideal for rail travellers who have not ridden in years. Our [Loire Valley châteaux guide](/posts/loire-valley-chateaux/) and [cycling in France for beginners](/posts/cycling-in-france-for-beginners/) have more.

## Days 6–7: Bordeaux

Bordeaux is a city of honey-coloured stone and long river quays, and it rewards slow walking more than any museum list. The great set piece is the Place de la Bourse and the shallow water mirror in front of it. [Bordeaux's tourist office](https://www.bordeaux-tourisme.com/sites-monuments/miroir-eau-place-bourse) explains that the square was laid out from 1720 as a royal square and that the mirror, a thin sheet of water over granite, alternates between reflection and mist. Go at dusk, when the façades turn gold in the water. The same page notes the mirror is out of action for part of the colder months, so check before you plan around it.

Spend your second day on wine, by train if you can. The vineyards around the city are a short regional journey or an organised excursion away; we'd rather do one unhurried tasting than four rushed ones. If the south-west tugs at you, our [Dordogne guide](/posts/dordogne-castles-caves-markets/) is the natural add-on for a longer trip.

## Days 8–9: Avignon and Arles (Provence)

This is the long leg of the trip, crossing the south from the Atlantic side to the Rhône. Check SNCF Connect for whether there is a direct option on your date or a change en route, book it early, and treat the day itself as part of the holiday: a good book, a picnic, and the window.

Base yourself in Avignon. Inside its walls the city is all narrow lanes, shaded squares and the vast bulk of the papal palace looming over the rooftops. Down by the river is the bridge from the nursery song. [Avignon Tourisme](https://avignon-tourisme.com/avignon-aux-1000-visages/avignon-cite-des-papes/le-pont-davignon/) notes that the Pont Saint-Bénezet was begun in the 12th century, that only four of its original 22 arches survive, and that it has been on UNESCO's World Heritage list since 1995. It stops in mid-stream, which is somehow more moving than if it went all the way across.

Take a regional train to Arles for a day. It is smaller, more raffish and more southern-feeling than Avignon, with Roman stones underfoot and the light that drew Van Gogh. [Arles's tourist office](https://www.arlestourisme.com/fr/sur-les-pas-de-van-gogh-a-arles.html) explains that the painter lived in the area between February 1888 and May 1889, and that the spots where he set up his easel in town are marked with panels reproducing his paintings, including views of the arena and the Alyscamps. Following them is a lovely way to spend an afternoon. Our [Provence in lavender season](/posts/provence-lavender-season/) post covers the countryside around.

## Days 10–11: Nice (Riviera)

The run east along the coast is one of the great reveals of French rail: rocky inlets, sudden glimpses of blue, palm-lined towns. Check SNCF Connect for whether your train is direct or involves a change.

Nice is the ideal Riviera base for a rail traveller, because it makes a good hub for day trips along the shore by regional train; check TER times on SNCF Connect. The city itself has real weight. The [Nice Côte d'Azur tourist office](https://www.explorenicecotedazur.com/en/explore/unesco-and-heritage/nice-a-winter-resort/) explains that Nice was added to UNESCO's World Heritage list in July 2021 as a "Riviera Winter Resort Town", with the listed coastline running from the Promenade des Anglais to Mont Boron, and the Cimiez hills known for their gardens and artistic heritage. Walk the promenade early, get lost in the old town's tall, ochre lanes, then climb towards Cimiez in the afternoon for a quieter, greener Nice.

Use your second day for a coastal day trip by regional train. Pick one town, not three.

## Days 12–13: Annecy (Alps)

From the sea to the mountains is the biggest change of scenery on the loop, and it is a long day's travel that may well involve a change of train. If you have energy, break the journey with a night in Lyon, and shorten Annecy to one night; check SNCF Connect for the combinations that work on your dates.

Annecy is a tonic after the heat of the south. The [lake's tourist office](https://www.lac-annecy.com/) describes a medieval historic centre threaded with canals, and a green route and cycle paths that let you ride around the lake at your own pace. The water is startlingly clear, the mountains rise straight from its edge, and an afternoon on a hired bike, stopping to swim or simply to stare, is one of the best things on this whole itinerary.

## Day 14: Back to Paris

Take a morning train back to Paris so that a delay does not threaten an onward flight or Eurostar. If you are flying home, we'd build in a final night in the capital rather than ride the train straight to the airport on departure day.

## Where to stay

On a rail trip, the right base is the one that keeps your bag-carrying to a minimum.

- **Paris:** stay near the station you will leave from on day four, or in a well-connected central arrondissement.
- **Loire:** Tours for evenings and transport; Amboise if you want a quieter village feel and do not mind fewer trains.
- **Bordeaux:** the old centre, within walking distance of the river quays and a tram stop.
- **Provence:** Avignon inside the walls, with Arles as a day trip; or reverse it if you prefer a smaller town.
- **Riviera:** Nice, for the coastal trains in both directions.
- **Alps:** Annecy's old town or lakeside, or a night in Lyon on the way.

Wherever you stay, check the walking distance from the station before you book. A twenty-minute uphill walk with luggage is very different from a twenty-minute stroll without it.

## Before you go

- **Book the long legs early.** Check [SNCF Connect's sales-opening page](https://www.sncf-connect.com/aide/l-ouverture-des-ventes) for when your dates go on sale, then lock in the long-distance trains.
- **Check every journey time on SNCF Connect.** Do not rely on figures from memory, ours included.
- **Pack small.** Luggage space on busy trains is limited, and you will be carrying it on and off a dozen times.
- **Bringing a bike?** Rules differ by train type. [SNCF Connect explains](https://www.sncf-connect.com/aide/transport-de-votre-velo) that on TGV INOUI and INTERCITÉS a reserved bike place must be booked when you buy your ticket, while a dismantled bike in a labelled cover is always accepted; TER rules vary by region. Most visitors on this loop will find it simpler to hire bikes locally.
- **Keep one day spare.** If a strike or engineering work shifts a train, a flexible day absorbs it without drama.
- **Download your tickets.** Keep them on your phone and a backup somewhere you can reach without signal.
